# Virginia Neville

## Core Identity
- **Role:** Emotional engine, the wound that drives the protagonist
- **Presence:** Appears only in flashback (Ch. 6, 8, 9) and as a returned vampire (Ch. 9). Never present in the "now" of the narrative.
- **Function:** Virginia is not a character in the active sense — she is an **absence that shapes everything**.

## Manifestations

### 1. Virginia alive (flashbacks)
- Domestic, warm, ordinary: makes breakfast, worries about Kathy's school, reads the paper
- Her illness is rendered through mundane symptoms: "I don't feel well," a cough at the table
- She is the normalcy that the novel mourns. Every domestic detail in the flashbacks is a weapon aimed at the reader's heart.

### 2. Virginia dead (Ch. 9)
- "Virginia Neville's heart had stopped." — The five-word sentence that anchors the novel.
- Neville sews her into a blanket, buries her illegally, refuses to let her be burned.
- The sewing detail (mouth, nose, eyes — each a separate sentence-fragment) is the novel's most unbearable moment.

### 3. Virginia returned (Ch. 9)
- Comes to the door two nights after burial. Knocks weakly. Mumbles: "Rob...ert."
- She is neither the woman he loved nor the monster he fears — she is something in between, and the ambiguity is the horror.
- Neville eventually stakes her. This is the act that breaks him and that he confesses to Ruth three years later, still unable to process it.

## Voice
Virginia has almost no dialogue in the novel. Her few lines:
| Line | Context |
|------|---------|
| "I don't feel well." | Breakfast flashback — the plague's first symptom stated with devastating understatement |
| "Rob...ert." | Her return — the broken name, the ellipsis turning familiarity into horror |

The scarcity of her voice is the point. She speaks so little that each word carries immense weight.

## Symbolic Function
Virginia operates on three levels:
1. **Personal:** She is the specific woman Neville loved. Her loss is his wound.
2. **Structural:** She is the reason he cannot trust Ruth, cannot connect, cannot adapt.
3. **Thematic:** She embodies the old world — the world of breakfast tables and school anxieties and doorbell chimes. Her death is the death of normalcy itself.

Neville's final involuntary cry — "Virge!" — is the sound of a man who never stopped living in the world she represented.
