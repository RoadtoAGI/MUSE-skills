# Ben Cortman

## Core Identity
- **Role:** Recurring antagonist, mirror character, structural rhythm device
- **Pre-plague:** Neville's neighbor and friend. Installed "How Dry I Am" doorbell chimes. Jovial.
- **Post-plague:** Vampire. Leads the nightly siege. His nightly call ("Come out, Neville!") is the novel's heartbeat.

## Function in the Narrative
Cortman is not a character in the psychological sense — he is a **structural element**. He appears at regular intervals to mark the passage of Neville's isolation. His call is the clock.

But he is also Neville's dark double:
- Both are trapped in compulsive repetition (Neville makes stakes; Cortman calls)
- Both are relics of the old world (Cortman is a "true" vampire; Neville is a "true" human)
- Both are destroyed by the new society (Cortman is machine-gunned; Neville is executed)

## Key Moments
| Moment | Chapter | Function |
|--------|---------|----------|
| "Come out, Neville!" (first call) | Ch. 1 | Establishes the siege pattern |
| Comatose in bed with Freda | Ch. 9 | Domestic uncanny — the vampire as sleeping neighbor |
| Attacks at the car window: "Neville, Neville!" | Ch. 5 | The friend become predator, using the intimate first name |
| Machine-gunned on the rooftop | Ch. 20 | Neville weeps for him — the only vampire death he mourns |

## Voice
Cortman has only one line, repeated throughout the novel:
> "Come out, Neville!"

This is one of the most effective single lines in horror fiction. Its power comes from:
- **Repetition:** It appears in almost every chapter. It becomes ambient, like tinnitus.
- **Familiarity:** It's a *neighbor* calling. The voice belongs to someone who used to invite Neville over for drinks.
- **Imperative mood:** It's a command, not a request. The friend now demands.
- **Name usage:** He uses Neville's last name — formal, impersonal, dehumanizing.

## Death and Its Meaning
Cortman's death in Chapter 20 is Neville's moral crisis. When the new society's squad machine-guns Cortman on a rooftop, Neville watches from his window and weeps. He mourns the vampire, not the human executioners. This reveals where Neville's true loyalty lies: not with humanity (old or new), but with the dying world. He is more kin to Cortman than to Ruth's society.
